# Self-Study Aerospace Engineering Curriculum: Bachelor's (4 Years) + Master's (2 Years)

Oct 19, 2026 · @cindeverest

## How to use this plan

Eight 15-week semesters (Years 1–3 core, Year 4 advanced), 5 courses each, ordered like a B.Sc./B.Tech aerospace degree: math and physics, then engineering science, then aero disciplines, then design.

- **Weekly load:** a 3-year bachelor's (180 ECTS) is roughly 4,500–5,400 hours, i.e. about 30–35 h/week. At 15 h/week, expect about 5 years instead.
- **Per course:** watch the lectures, read the matching textbook chapter, solve every posted problem set before looking at solutions, then sit the posted exam under timed conditions.
- **Gatekeeping:** do not start a semester until the previous semester's math is solid; aero courses depend on it.
- **Format key:** (V) = video lectures; (N) = lecture notes, problem sets and exams, no video.
- **Links:** every course page below was checked on 19 Oct 2026. If one moves, search the course number or title on the platform.
- **Books:** marked (free) are open-access; the rest are standard textbooks (library or purchase).

## Master's level (2 years, after Years 1–4)

The Master's stage has 4 semesters: a common graduate core (M1), one specialisation track (M2-S1), then a thesis (M2-S2).

- **Prerequisite:** all bachelor-level courses above, especially linear algebra, ODEs, control, CFD and FEM.
- **Load:** 4 courses per semester. Graduate courses need about 8–10 h/week each, so \~35 h/week in total.
- **Format:** many graduate MIT courses are notes + problem sets only (N). Pair them with the textbook and work every problem.
- **Output:** a Master's is judged by research. Aim to finish with one paper submitted to a conference or posted as a preprint.

## Master's Year 2, Semester 2 — Thesis

The thesis is a full semester (\~30 ECTS equivalent) of original work in your track.

1. **Question:** one falsifiable research question, narrowed from your literature log.
2. **Method:** analysis, simulation (CFD/FEM/dynamics) or experiment, with a written verification and validation plan before you run anything.
3. **Reproducibility:** public code and data repository, fixed versions, scripts that regenerate every figure.
4. **Results:** uncertainty or sensitivity analysis on every key number; compare with published data.
5. **Write-up:** thesis document (\~60–80 pages) plus a short paper version submitted to a conference or posted as a preprint.
6. **Review:** ask a practising engineer or academic to critique the draft before submission.

## Practical work (alongside the courses)

One build or analysis project per year turns lectures into engineering skill.

- **Year 1:** design, build and trim a hand-launched glider; log flight results.
- **Year 2:** size an RC aircraft from your Airplane Performance calculations; model it in OpenVSP and compare predicted vs measured performance.
- **Year 3:** capstone UAV or CubeSat conceptual design report (requirements, sizing, aero, propulsion, structures, stability, trade studies).
- **Code as you go (Python):** standard atmosphere, drag polar, range/endurance, Tsiolkovsky equation, two-body orbit propagator, longitudinal flight modes. Validate each against textbook examples.

## Credentials and limits

Self-study builds knowledge, not a degree; most aerospace employers and engineering registration bodies expect an accredited degree.

- **NPTEL** offers proctored exams with certificates (small fee) for its courses — the closest route to verifiable credentials with free content. Coursera/edX certificates are paid.
- A public **portfolio** (GitHub code, design reports, built hardware) is the strongest evidence of competence for a self-taught engineer.
- Keep records of every course and exam score; part-time or distance degree programmes may credit prior learning later.
